The biggest surprise was that the simplest model was the strongest. A TF-IDF plus logistic-regression baseline scored macro-F1 0.590 on responses alone, above my best neural model (CNN, mean 0.554 over three seeds) and well above the BiLSTM (0.469). I had expected the networks to win, so I treated this as a result to explain rather than hide. The likely reasons are that the training set is small (5,600 rows), that truncation at 180 tokens throws away information the linear model still sees, and that much of the signal is in specific words and phrases that n-gram counts capture directly. Two other results were equally clear and stable across seeds. The prompt alone is useless for identifying the family (macro-F1 0.18 to 0.20, at chance), which makes sense because every Arena prompt is answered by two different models, and adding the prompt to the response did not help (CNN 0.554 to 0.545, BiLSTM 0.469 to 0.397). The families were not equally easy to spot either: in the seed-42 models Claude was the most identifiable (CNN F1 0.80), while ChatGLM (0.40) and the merged Llama-derived group (0.43) were hardest, which fits the fact that Llama-derived mixes several fine-tunes.

Explaining why the classifiers work was less clean than showing that they do. Newline count was the most important feature in the gradient-boosting analysis (permutation importance 0.174), and removing markdown, lists and line breaks lowered the neural scores (CNN 0.554 to 0.446, BiLSTM 0.469 to 0.426), but the size of that drop was unstable. The CNN's formatting-stripped score ranged from 0.384 to 0.490 across the three seeds, so the average drop of about 11 points is far less certain than a single run suggested; had I stopped at one seed I would have reported a drop of 7 points. The TF-IDF model barely changed (0.590 to 0.566), and all models stayed well above chance, so wording carries most of the signal and layout adds some of it. I also cannot say how much of either is true model style rather than the kinds of prompts each model happened to receive. The leave-one-domain-out experiment points the same way: performance dropped by about 0.07 macro-F1 on unseen task types but stayed well above chance. Those drops are confounded by training-set size (holding out the large "general" group removes most of the data), the domains come from keyword rules, and RQ3 used one seed, so I treat it as suggestive only.

On the process side, long runs were interrupted more than once, which taught me to make experiments recoverable. I ran each seed as its own job, rebuilt the seed-42 test predictions from saved checkpoints and checked that they reproduced the reported accuracies exactly (0.56375 and 0.463125) before running RQ3 on top of them, and re-ran only the conditions that were missing. The code details that mattered most were the ones that silently change results: masking padding in max-pooling, packing sequences for the LSTM, capping the prompt in the combined condition, and grouping both answers to one question into the same split. With more time I would use more than three seeds (the ablation shows three is still noisy), balance the families more carefully, split the Llama-derived group by checkpoint, test on a more recent dataset that includes current model families, and use real task labels instead of keyword rules for RQ3.
